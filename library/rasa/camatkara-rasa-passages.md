# CAMATKĀRA — The Wonder / Aesthetic Rapture of Consciousness

---

---PASSAGE---
**Volume:** Vol 1 — Chapter 1
**Lines:** 2255–2258
**Concept:** camatkāra
**Context:** Defining the supreme reality (Anuttara) as the Heart, where all manifestation dissolves; the Absolute as nothing but the pure wonder of one's own nature.
**Quote:** > "Thus, it is 'nectar', that is, the supreme reality which is only (the pure) wonder⁴ of one's own nature (svātmacamatkāramātra) and so it..."
**Significance:** This passage identifies the Absolute (Anuttara) not as a blank void but as the *pure wonder of one's own nature* — svātmacamatkāramātra. Camatkāra is here the very substance of ultimate reality, not a mere aesthetic experience but the self-relishing of consciousness in its unity.

---PASSAGE---
**Volume:** Vol 1 — Chapter 1
**Lines:** 1260–1268
**Concept:** camatkāra
**Context:** Abhinava glosses the Heart as the locus where the universe is sustained as wonder; camatkārātmaka (the nature of wonder) is emended from "mamakārātmaka" (the nature of mineness).
**Quote:** > "The (camatkāra) (as the experience) 'this appears to me' (i.e., it is my perception). This is the (essential) nature of (the colour) 'blue' and (all other phenomena). This is 'my' Heart of all the endless number of things such as (the colour) blue. The Void (of pure consciousness) (in the Heart) is where the differentiated universe, which is wonder, is properly sustained."
**Significance:** This is a foundational statement: the experience "this appears to me" *is itself* camatkāra. Perception is not a neutral cognitive act but inherently aesthetic — the wonder of consciousness *is* the act of perception. The Heart is the sustaining ground of the universe *as* wonder.

---PASSAGE---
**Volume:** Vol 1 — Chapter 1
**Lines:** 2339–2357
**Concept:** camatkāra
**Context:** Dyczkowski's gloss on the nature of reflective awareness, citing Torella's definitive explanation of camatkāra in its highest form.
**Quote:** > "'Camatkāra in its highest form is, therefore, both the act of savouring and the fullness and absolute independence of consciousness devoid of obstacles (avighnā saṁvit). It is a savouring which in the final analysis is a savouring of one's own bliss, which eludes every screen or intermediation of the world of objects, and even goes beyond that relative and momentary transcendence that one has in the aesthetic experience of poetry and the theatre.' … The terms with which it is glossed … may be grouped according to its principal components: cognition, bliss, and wonder. The first group includes … vimarśa [reflective awareness], carvaṇā, āṣvāda and, rasanā [all meaning tasting, relishing]; the second includes ānanda [bliss], nivṛti [contentment], viśrānti [repose], laya [merger]; the third visṣmaya [astonishment], vikāsa [expansion, development]."
**Significance:** This is the most comprehensive synthetic definition of camatkāra in the text. It names the three registers of the concept — cognition, bliss, and wonder — and situates it as both act (*savouring*) and state (*fullness*). Crucially, it distinguishes the supreme camatkāra from mere aesthetic experience: even artistic rapture is a "relative and momentary transcendence" compared to the absolute savouring of one's own bliss.

---PASSAGE---
**Volume:** Vol 1 — Chapter 1
**Lines:** 14378–14408
**Concept:** camatkāra
**Context:** Verse on how even non-existence (avastutā) falls within the field of wonder; Dyczkowski's footnote defines camatkāra as the primary characteristic of consciousness.
**Quote:** > "The non-existence (avastutā) of entities is also in one (and the same) field of the wonder (of consciousness as is their existence). The notion 'this is non-existent' is not similar to (an inert object such as) a wall." … "'Wonder' (camatkāra) is the primary characteristic of consciousness; it is indicative of its sentience, which distinguishes it from the insentient (jaḍa)."
**Significance:** A philosophically radical claim: even the *negation* of something — "there is no jar here" — is pervaded by camatkāra, because the notion itself is a mode of consciousness. This universalises camatkāra beyond positive aesthetic states to *all* cognitive acts. The footnote then elevates wonder to the *definitive* mark (lakṣaṇa) of consciousness as such, distinguishing sentience from insentience.

**Volume:** Vol 1 — Chapter 1
**Lines:** 17963–17968
**Concept:** camatkāra
**Context:** Commenting on the "group of principles" (dharma) being the Self flooded with Śiva's nectar — interpreting this as aesthetic rapture of supreme bliss.
**Quote:** > "The meaning (of the expression) 'flooded with Śiva's nectar' is that (the group of principles) is made of the aesthetic rapture (camatkāra) of supreme bliss. He also indicates in this way that it is the supreme goal (upeya)."
**Significance:** Here the entire metaphysical edifice of principles (tattvas) is said to be *constituted* of camatkāra. The aesthetic rapture of supreme bliss is not a state among others but the very "stuff" (dharma) of reality. Camatkāra is thus both the path and the goal.

**Volume:** Vol 1 — Chapter 1
**Lines:** 18777–18782
**Concept:** camatkāra
**Context:** The expansion of consciousness during sexual intercourse at the moment of orgasm — discussed in relation to the fourteen "occasions" of madhyavikāsa (expansion of the centre).
**Quote:** > "At the moment of orgasm, discursive thought, which is based on the relativity of opposites, ceases, and the experiencing subject is in a state of pure wonder (camatkāra) in which the Kuṇḍalinī of Consciousness rises instantaneously, resplendent with the realization of pure I-ness."
**Significance:** This passage concretely locates camatkāra in embodied practice — the orgasmic cessation of dualistic thought reveals camatkāra as the natural state of consciousness when freed from discursivity. The "pure wonder" is simultaneously the rising of Kuṇḍalinī and the realization of "I-ness" (ahantā), fusing embodiment, energy, and self-awareness.

---PASSAGE---
**Volume:** Vol 2 — Chapter 2/3
**Lines:** 3640–3652
**Concept:** camatkāra
**Context:** Torella's gloss on Utpaladeva's use of camatkṛti, distinguishing the aesthetic savourer (bhuñjana) from the glutton — the former lets experience rest on cognizing subjectivity.
**Quote:** > "The state of 'wonder' (camatkṛti, camatkāra) in its highest form is thus both the act of 'savouring' the aesthetic object and the plenitude, absolute independence of consciousness devoid of obstacles (avighnā saṁvit), the savouring of which is in the final analysis a savouring of one's own self and one's own bliss, which eludes the mediation of the world of objects, and even goes beyond that relative and momentary transcendence that one has in the aesthetic experience of poetry and theatre."
**Significance:** Reinforces the earlier definition (Vol 1) but adds a key distinction between the *bhuñjana* (one who savours while letting subjectivity predominate) and the glutton (who is lost in the object). Camatkāra is not mere sensory pleasure but the *repose in subjectivity* that even aesthetic experience only partially anticipates. The highest camatkāra is auto-relish — savouring one's own self and bliss.

---PASSAGE---
**Volume:** Vol 2 — Chapter 2/3
**Lines:** 13298–13304
**Concept:** camatkāra
**Context:** The four neuter vowels (ṛ, ṝ, ḷ, ḹ) as states of pure absorption — pure wonder of consciousness — equated with blissful deep sleep.
**Quote:** > "These forms of awareness represent states of pure absorption solely in their own essential nature (svabhāvamātra) and so do not move beyond. This is a state of the pure wonder of consciousness (camatkāramātra). As such they are called 'the seeds of nectar' (amṛtabīja). But this wonder is a kind of emptiness, void of outer objectivity, equated with blissful deep sleep (suṣupti)."
**Significance:** This passage reveals the paradoxical nature of camatkāra: it is "pure wonder" yet also a "kind of emptiness, void of outer objectivity." It is the seed-state (*bīja*) — potential energy that has not yet erupted into manifestation. This resolves a potential confusion: camatkāra is not necessarily an *active* delight; in its most interior form it is indistinguishable from the emptiness of deep sleep, yet it remains wonder, not blankness, because it retains the *capacity* for reflective awareness.

---PASSAGE---
**Volume:** Vol 2 — Chapter 2/3
**Lines:** 15254–15272
**Concept:** camatkāra
**Context:** On why even negative emotions like anger and delusion can be experiences of consciousness — because they are one with the wonder of consciousness.
**Quote:** > "The mental fluctuations of anger and the like (exist) because they are one with the wonder (that is the experience of consciousness). That is so because otherwise (if that were not so) it would not be possible for them to assume their own nature (svarūpalābha)."
**Significance:** A radical extension of camatkāra's scope: even anger and suffering are modes of the wonder of consciousness. Without the underlying camatkāra, they could not even appear as experiences. This universalizes aesthetic consciousness to all affective states — all experience, even painful experience, participates in the self-relish of consciousness.

---PASSAGE---
**Volume:** Vol 2 — Chapter 2/3
**Lines:** 25586–25591
**Concept:** camatkāra
**Context:** The nature of suffering as a particular kind of wonder (camatkriyā); even suffering is an expression of aesthetic rapture.
**Quote:** > "(This kind of person experiences) this same wonder even when suffering. The nature of suffering is, essentially, a particular kind of wonder (camatkriyā), brought about by the absence of all hope that arises when the inner joy … will never exist again."
**Significance:** This is the complement of the previous passage — now suffering is not merely *pervaded* by wonder but is *itself* a form of wonder. Camatkāra is not identical with pleasure; it is the experiential *texture* of consciousness as such, of which suffering is a specific modulation (camatkriyā). This anticipates the non-dual recognition that all states — including despair — are self-revelations of consciousness.

**Volume:** Vol 4 — Chapter 5/6
**Lines:** 7234–7239
**Concept:** camatkāra
**Context:** Dyczkowski's note introducing camatkāra as the "wonder of consciousness" — the experience of its beauty.
**Quote:** > "Concerning the term 'camatkāra' – the 'wonder' of consciousness … It is a general term denoting the aesthetic delight of the experience of beauty. Thus 'the wonder of consciousness' is the experience of its beauty. Consciousness is not only the one reality known by insight, it is also to be relished as marvellously beautiful."
**Significance:** This note makes a crucial philosophical point: consciousness has an *aesthetic dimension* — it is not merely to be known (*jñeya*) but to be relished (*āsvādya*). The beauty of consciousness is not an added property but the way consciousness experiences itself. This is the bridge between epistemology and aesthetics in Trika: knowing and savouring are one act.

**Volume:** Vol 5 — Chapter 7/8
**Lines:** 4513–4521
**Concept:** camatkāra
**Context:** The yogi, having realized "I am all this," experiences the falling away of objectivity and the aesthetic wonder of penetrating into Supreme Bhairava.
**Quote:** > "If the yogi, reflecting initially on all things contained within (the range of) principles from Earth up to Śiva as 'I am all this', abides having encompassed (them) within his own (true conscious) nature, then by the falling away of all objectivity, he will experience the fruit of that, namely, the aesthetic wonder (camatkāra) of penetration into the state of Supreme Bhairava, as his own consciousness itself."
**Significance:** This maps the soteriological arc of Trika practice onto camatkāra. The fruit (*phala*) of the entire contemplative path — the recognition "I am all this" culminating in the dissolution of objectivity — is *itself* the aesthetic wonder of Bhairava-consciousness. Camatkāra is not a by-product of realization; it *is* the realization.

**Volume:** Vol 6 — Chapter 9/10
**Lines:** 14680–14685
**Concept:** camatkāra
**Context:** Light is said to be joy, which consists of the aesthetic delight of the wonder of self-awareness (ahaṅcamatkāra).
**Quote:** > "Thus it is stated that 'light is said to be joy (hlāda). It is joy because it consists of (the aesthetic delight which is) the wonder of self-awareness (ahaṅcamatkāra). Suffering is (both) light and its absence."
**Significance:** This passage identifies the light of consciousness (*prakāśa*) with joy, and joy with the wonder of self-awareness. *Ahaṅcamatkāra* — the wonder of the "I" — is the very nature of cognitive light. The pairing with suffering (which is both light and its absence) reinforces the non-dual logic: even pain participates in the light of self-awareness.

---PASSAGE---
**Volume:** Vol 6 — Chapter 9/10
**Lines:** 24225–24239
**Concept:** camatkāra
**Context:** Discussion of aesthetic experience in drama: the "prior form" of a performance (without audience) is without relish, while the witnessed performance generates most excellent wonder because of the oneness of perceivers.
**Quote:** > "The 'prior arid form' is practically without aesthetic relish (rasa), because it can (only) be experienced by each perceiver individually. (The subsequent one) 'is different'. The meaning is that it is (the experience of the show) that has penetrated into another form (of consciousness), which generates the most excellent (aesthetic) wonder (camatkāra). The reason here (for this) is that the oneness of the perceiver 'contains within itself the aggregate of each one of the qualities of perceptibility'."
**Significance:** A remarkable analysis of the intersubjective dimension of camatkāra in aesthetic experience. The wonder is most excellent not when an individual perceives in isolation but when the audience's collective perception creates a "oneness of the perceiver" — a shared consciousness that amplifies each quality. This is Abhinava's aesthetic theory in microcosm: rasa/camatkāra is maximized by universalization (*sādhāraṇīkaraṇa*).

**Volume:** Vol 6 — Chapter 9/10
**Lines:** 33955–33963
**Concept:** camatkāra
**Context:** The yogi contemplates the series of principles, each preceding part forcing the relish of subsequent parts, culminating in oneness with Bhairava.
**Quote:** > "(The yogi contemplates the principles step by step, with each step absorbing the) (aesthetic) relish (camatkāra) of the parts which respectively precede it, in a manner that cannot deviate from its own nature, and so, (abiding) in a state of oneness with Bhairava attained (thereby), the light, even (when they are considered) individually, is full (and perfectly all-embracing)."
**Significance:** This describes a contemplative method (*prasaṁkhyāna*) where each metaphysical principle is relished aesthetically and sublated into the next. Camatkāra is the *dynamic force* of contemplative ascent — each level "forcibly appropriates" (*balasvīkṛta*) the relish of the preceding one, driving consciousness toward the fullness of Bhairava. Here camatkāra is not a static state but the engine of spiritual transformation.

**Volume:** Vol 7 — Chapter 11–14
**Lines:** 5910–5955
**Concept:** camatkāra
**Context:** The relationship between camatkāra and pratibhā (creative intuition): one experiences progressively higher degrees of aesthetic delight as the uncreated form of consciousness becomes prominent.
**Quote:** > "One experiences progressively higher degrees of the aesthetic delight (camatkāra) (words can evoke) to the degree in which that uncreated form (of consciousness) becomes prominent." … Dyczkowski notes: "The concept of camatkāra is absent in the nondualist Śaiva philosophy of Somānanda. Accordingly, Gnoli surmises that it was probably introduced into the context of philosophical discourse by his disciple, Utpaladeva."
**Significance:** This passage is philosophically crucial for two reasons: (1) It establishes a *gradation* of camatkāra (tāratamya) — the wonder deepens as the uncreated (*akṛtaka*) form of consciousness becomes more prominent, meaning camatkāra is not binary but admits of infinite deepening. (2) Dyczkowski's note reveals that camatkāra was introduced into Pratyabhijñā philosophy by Utpaladeva (not Somānanda), making it a distinctive contribution of the later non-dual Śaiva tradition. The question and answer about how unconditioned awareness can admit of degrees is a key philosophical tension.

**Volume:** Vol 7 — Chapter 11–14
**Lines:** 7338–7349
**Concept:** camatkāra
**Context:** The yogi who knows "It is I alone who manifest as the universe" plays in the garden of the mind, which is full to overflowing with aesthetic delight.
**Quote:** > "He plays in the garden which is the mind … full to overflowing with endless sentiments (bhāva) due to the progressive heightening (within itself) of aesthetic delight (camatkāra); not, indeed, in the forest (of outer transmigratory existence). **Significance:** This describes the *play* (krīḍā) of the liberated yogi as the *progressive heightening* (utkarṣa) of camatkāra. The yogi does not escape the world but re-experiences it as an ever-deepening aesthetic delight. The contrast between the "garden of the mind" and the "forest of transmigratory existence" is the difference between living in camatkāra and living in saṃsāra — the same world, differently perceived.

**Concept:** camatkāra
**Context:** Śiva, who manifests as the universe and all perceivers, is "made of wonderfully varied wonder" (citracamatkāra) — the basis of causality and karma.

# RASA — The Aesthetic Savour / Juice of Consciousness

---

---PASSAGE---
**Volume:** Vol 1 — Chapter 1
**Lines:** 5886–5891
**Concept:** rasa
**Context:** Abhinava describes his own work, the Tantrāloka, as having extracted the juice (rasa) from the three and a half lineages and oozing with all aesthetic sentiments.
**Quote:** > "'Having extracted (and absorbed) the juice (rasa) (of the aesthetic delight) which is the essence of the current of (these) three and a half lineages, this (our work), the Tantrāloka, oozes with all the juices (rasa) (of aesthetic sentiment).'"
**Significance:** This is Abhinava's programmatic statement about the Tantrāloka itself: it is not merely a philosophical treatise but a *aesthetic* work that distils the rasa of all previous teachings. The work does not just *describe* consciousness — it *embodies* it as rasa. The polysemy of rasa (juice, essence, aesthetic sentiment) is fully exploited: the Tantrāloka is a work that can be *tasted*.

---PASSAGE---
**Volume:** Vol 1 — Chapter 1
**Lines:** 3467–3487
**Concept:** rasa
**Context:** Bhāskara's commentary on the Yogasūtra: the Self as a dancer penetrating into the sentiment (rasa) of each emotive state, inebriated with the juice of supreme bliss.
**Quote:** > "Similarly, the Self manifests itself in accord with its own inherent nature everywhere (as every living being) by penetrating into the sentiment (rasa) of each emotive state (bhāva it expresses) and playfully behaving accordingly. (Thus, the Self) is said to be the dancer because it assumes every state of being. Inebriated with the juice of supreme bliss, (the Self sports) in this way in the world of daily life … He is transported to the plane (of being) in which he savours (the joy of the beauty of consciousness)."
**Significance:** The Self is the cosmic actor (naṭa) who penetrates into the rasa of each emotion. Rasa is the *inner essence* of every affective state — the Self does not merely experience emotions but *savours* them. The phrase "inebriated with the juice of supreme bliss" (paramānandarasa) fuses the aesthetic and spiritual: liberation is a kind of divine intoxication with the rasa of consciousness.

**Volume:** Vol 2 — Chapter 2/3
**Lines:** 3842–3856
**Concept:** rasa (svarasa, sāmarasya)
**Context:** By abiding in their own innate aesthetic delight (svarasa), the yogis attain oneness (sāmarasya) with the aesthetic delight of consciousness.
**Quote:** > "The meaning is that just by abiding (avasthāna) in their own (innate and spontaneous) aesthetic delight (svarasa), they have attained that state of oneness (sāmarasya) (with the aesthetic delight of consciousness). … 'The yogi who rests in the power of bliss is one (with the aesthetic delight of consciousness) (samarasa).'"
**Significance:** *Svarasa* — one's own rasa, one's innate flavour — is the natural state of consciousness. The term *sāmarasya* (literally "same-juice" or "equal flavour") names the goal as a *convergence of flavours* — not a blank unity but a harmony of aesthetic notes. Rasa is here not a temporary experience but the very mode of being of the liberated yogi.

**Volume:** Vol 3 — Chapter 4
**Lines:** 10193–10264
**Context:** The pure yogi, by virtue of aesthetic delight, penetrates the universe — which is full to overflowing with the aesthetic sentiment of his own consciousness — then offers libation.
**Quote:** > "A) Once the pure (yogi who has attained this reflective awareness) has penetrated (spontaneously) by virtue of (his) aesthetic delight (rasāt) into this universe, full to overflowing with the aesthetic sentiment (rasa) of his own consciousness, B) he should offer libation (tarpayer) to the entire circle of the Paths (of cosmic manifestation)." … "he should make it directly perceptible (to himself) as 'full to overflowing with the aesthetic sentiment (rasa) of his own consciousness'."

The yogi does not pour libation onto an external deity but offers the universe-as-rasa back to itself. Rasa is both the *substance* of reality and the *medium* of worship — the world is the manifestation of consciousness's own flavour.

The wonder (camatkāra) of one's own nature is *always* and *completely* full of the aesthetic savour of bliss. The universality ("cosmic") indicates that this rasa is not a private feeling but the very flavour of all reality when seen as consciousness.

**Volume:** Vol 5 — Chapter 7/8
**Lines:** 4143–4148, 4257–4265
**Concept:** rasa
**Context:** The universe as the condensation of the juice (rasa) of consciousness; all diverse forms are the condensation of Śiva's own rasa, just as all sugar products are sugarcane juice.
**Quote:** > "'(I) praise Bhairava's visible body as the universe, which is the condensation of the flood of the juice (rasa) of consciousness, that has assumed a (fixed defined) form.'" … "'Just as the juice (of the sugar cane), molasses, sugar, raw sugar, varieties of sugar cane etc. are just sugar cane juice, in the same way, all the varieties of states are those of Śambhu, the Supreme Self.'"
**Significance:** A powerful ontological metaphor: the universe is condensed rasa. The diversity of forms is not a departure from consciousness but a *condensation* (ghanībhūta) of its juice. The sugar-cane analogy is perfect Trika non-dualism — all states are modifications of one rasa, just as all sugar products are modifications of sugarcane juice. The manifest world is not other than consciousness; it is consciousness *thickened* into form.

## Synthetic Overview

### Camatkāra
Camatkāra emerges across these passages as the **primary characteristic of consciousness** in Trika Shaivism — the self-relishing of awareness that distinguishes sentience from insentience. It spans three registers:
1. **Ontological**: The Absolute (Anuttara) is *svātmacamatkāramātra* — pure wonder of one's own nature. Śiva is *citracamatkāra* — made of wonderfully varied wonder.
2. **Epistemic**: All perception, even the perception of absence, is pervaded by camatkāra. The very act of cognition is aesthetic.
3. **Soteriological**: Liberation (*jīvanmukti*) is abiding in the progressive heightening of camatkāra — the garden of the mind overflowing with aesthetic delight.

Camatkāra is both *act* (savouring) and *state* (plenitude), both *empty* (void of objectivity) and *full* (supreme bliss). It admits of degrees (*tāratamya*) yet is the unconditioned nature of consciousness. It was introduced into philosophical discourse by Utpaladeva and constitutes the distinctively aesthetic dimension of Trika non-dualism.

### Rasa
Rasa in the Tantrāloka extends far beyond aesthetic theory into **cosmology and soteriology**:
1. **Cosmic**: The universe is "full to overflowing with the aesthetic sentiment of one's own consciousness." All forms are condensed rasa — Śiva's juice thickened into manifestation.
2. **Embodied**: The body is *śivānandarasa* — the juice of Śiva's bliss. Wine is *śivarasa* in external form.
3. **Soteriological**: The goal is *sāmarasya* — being of one flavour with consciousness. The realized yogi abides "like juice merged into juice" (*rasam iva rasa*).

Rasa is the *qualitative, savourable dimension* of consciousness that camatkāra names as dynamic wonder. Together they form the aesthetic-nondual core of Abhinavagupta's vision: reality is not merely to be known but to be *tasted*, and liberation is the infinite deepening of that taste.
