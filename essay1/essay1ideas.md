# Research dossier: Comedy, selfhood, rebirth, consciousness and information

The strongest discovery is that the connections you're making are not merely poetic analogies. Several of them correspond to established philosophical arguments, and some have surprisingly close parallels in contemporary cognitive science.

However, you're combining three different types of claims: things Buddhist texts explicitly teach, modern interpretations of those teachings, and hypotheses about biology and information processing. Distinguishing them actually makes the connections more interesting.

## 1. Ñāṇananda's whirlpool: remarkably close to your model of the self

Primary source: Bhikkhu Ñāṇananda, Nibbāna and the Fire Simile, printed pp. 9–11.

This is the most directly relevant material I found.

Ñāṇananda compares an individual caught in saṃsāra to a whirlpool forming within a river or ocean.

A whirlpool has a recognisable structure, boundaries, apparent persistence and a location. Yet there isn't a separate object called a whirlpool sitting inside the water.

The whirlpool is a pattern of activity maintained by conditions.

He explains that a current struggling against the main flow can circle back repeatedly, creating a vortex.

His crucial phrase is:

> "this whirlpool or eddy of a person"

Ñāṇananda explicitly connects that vortex to consciousness and nāmarūpa (name-and-form), mutually conditioned in the process of dependent arising.

A whirlpool is identifiable and causally effective without being an independently existing substance.

Your model of an identity continually recreated by habitual craving is very close to his analogy.

But there's an important distinction. Ñāṇananda's vortex is not merely a succession of emotional states. It represents a self-sustaining pattern of becoming conditioned by ignorance, craving and the mutual dependence of consciousness and name-and-form.

His explanation of liberation is correspondingly radical: when the conditions that sustain the vortex cease, the vortex ceases. The water doesn't have to travel to another location.

This is why the traditional question of where an enlightened person goes after death is, in his interpretation, misconceived.

He says:

> "How ridiculous it is to conclude that the fire goes somewhere when it goes out."

This gives your idea a more exact philosophical formulation:

- Saṃsāra is a conditioned process rather than an independently existing object.
- Personal identity is sustained by mutually dependent physical, perceptual and psychological conditions.
- Craving and identification contribute to the continuation of that process.
- Nibbāna is associated with the cessation of the conditions maintaining bondage, not the movement of a soul to a new destination.

Ñāṇananda also explicitly insists that liberation is realisable during life. It isn't merely an experience waiting for biological death.

Where your interpretation goes beyond him: saying that nibbāna must always already be present as an underlying state of pure consciousness is not the same as saying that the cessation of craving is accessible here and now. The former is closer to certain nondual Śaiva and Mahāyāna interpretations; the latter is closer to Ñāṇananda's particular early Buddhist reading.

This distinction becomes especially important when comparing him with the Śiva Sūtras.

## 2. An unexpected discovery: Ñāṇananda actually uses the cinema to explain reality

Nibbāna: The Mind Stilled, Volume I, Sermon 5, printed pp. 108–110.

This is an unusually exact parallel with your central idea.

Ñāṇananda discusses what happens when somebody enters a cinema. For a while, the spectator forgets they're watching a constructed drama. The performance becomes a seemingly real world.

He then makes this startling claim:

> "a film show or drama is produced, in the last analysis, by the spectator himself."

He is not denying that actors, cameras and film exist. He's arguing that the experienced drama depends on the spectator's own mental contributions.

He specifically invokes saṅkhāra (formations, fabrications or preparations) and āsava (deep-seated influxes or taints).

For him, the spectator brings the dispositions required to become absorbed in the representation.

Then comes an even more interesting experiment.

Imagine a film playing in a completely dark cinema. The audience is immersed in the imagery.

Suddenly somebody opens the doors and lets daylight flood the room.

The colours fade. The illusion loses its grip. What seemed like an independently compelling world is revealed as a projection dependent on particular conditions.

He compares that disruption to the appearance of wisdom during ordinary experience.

This is much closer to your analogy between a comedian constructing an experience and consciousness constructing a world than I expected to find in the original sermons.

There's another passage in Sermon 1 where he examines how a child learns to recognise a rubber ball. He distinguishes conventional naming from the more basic perceptual processes involved in recognising an object.

The interesting part is his insistence that nāma-rūpa is not reducible to a mere verbal label: feeling, perception, intention, contact and attention are all involved.

So your laptop analogy has two levels:

1. The laptop is designated through conventional language and practical recognition.
2. The very experience of a recognisable object already depends on sensory and cognitive conditions.

The latter is much more radical than simply saying that a laptop is an assembly of components.

The ancient chariot analogy in the Milindapañha, which I'll return to below, provides an even closer parallel to your physical deconstruction experiment.

## 3. Your claim about rebirth is a real Buddhist interpretation—but not the only one

Your proposition is that rebirth means the continual construction of an identity through craving, identification and habit.

There is a particularly close modern Theravāda source for this: Buddhadāsa Bhikkhu.

### Buddhadāsa: the repeated birth of the ego

In Heart-Wood from the Bo Tree and his teachings on Dhamma language, Buddhadāsa distinguishes ordinary physical birth from the psychological birth of an ego.

He writes:

> "Birth here does not mean the birth from a mother's womb but the birth in the vastness of the mind."

In this interpretation, when an experience generates attachment to "I" or "mine," a self-centred identity is produced.

This is very close to what you're calling moment-to-moment rebirth.

Crucially, it is more specific than the claim that every thought is rebirth. For Buddhadāsa, the significant event is identification and clinging, not the mere presence of mental activity.

A thought can arise without necessarily generating egoic identification.

This provides an important distinction between ordinary cognition and the compulsive creation of a self around cognition.

### The competing interpretation: literal rebirth

But your statement that saṃsāra might not involve physical realms needs an important qualification.

Early Buddhist texts repeatedly and explicitly describe rebirth following biological death.

For example:

- MN 38, the Mahātaṇhāsaṅkhaya Sutta, discusses conception, dependent consciousness and the process by which repeated birth occurs.
- MN 130, the Devadūta Sutta, describes beings appearing in different destinations after bodily death, including hell.
- SN 44.9, the Kutūhalasālā Sutta, uses the image of a fire sustained by fuel to explain how craving sustains rebirth.

The early texts therefore cannot accurately be summarised as teaching only psychological rebirth.

The relationship is better expressed as follows:

| Interpretation | What is reborn? | Status |
|---|---|---|
| Traditional Theravāda | A causally continuous process, without an eternal transmigrating soul, takes birth after death | Mainstream traditional doctrine |
| Moment-to-moment analysis | Patterns of becoming and identification arise during this very life | Supported as a contemplative application |
| Buddhadāsa's radical psychological reading | The ego is repeatedly born through attachment to "I" and "mine" | Explicit modern Buddhist interpretation |
| Your proposed biological model | Conditioned psychological strategies reproduce themselves through habits, neural processes and behaviour | A contemporary synthesis, not an established canonical doctrine |

Importantly, some traditional interpreters also accept moment-to-moment becoming. The interpretations are not necessarily mutually exclusive. Thanissaro Bhikkhu explicitly discusses both levels in his introduction to MN 38.

### Karma: your habit example is quite close, with one correction

In AN 6.63, the Nibbedhika Sutta, the Buddha says:

> "Intention, I tell you, is kamma."

This is crucial.

Karma is not simply whatever consequences follow from an action. It is primarily intentional action, expressed through body, speech and mind, with consequences conditioned by many interacting factors.

Your example of somebody conditioned to respond violently is therefore a strong illustration of how intentions, habits and circumstances can reinforce one another.

But saying that the resulting act was literally outside the person's control is too deterministic.

Buddhist ethics is especially interested in how conditioned patterns can be recognised and transformed. Present intentions become additional conditions; they aren't simply the passive execution of an unalterable historical program.

Your analogy with addiction and habitual reinforcement is useful, but it explains one dimension of saṃsāra, not the entirety of traditional Buddhist cosmology.

### Nibbāna: cessation isn't the same as a perpetually existing inner self

There is a striking canonical passage in Udāna 8.3:

> "There is, monks, an unborn–unbecome–unmade–unfabricated."

The Buddha presents the unconditioned as what makes escape from conditioned processes possible.

This provides stronger textual support for your intuition that liberation isn't simply a reward manufactured in the future.

But it does not establish that the individual has secretly possessed an eternal, changeless consciousness throughout all their lives.

That stronger claim requires a different philosophical argument.

### The Buddha's refusal to answer whether the self exists

Your intuition that Buddhism rejects both straightforward self-existence and straightforward self-nonexistence has an exact source.

In SN 44.10, Vacchagotta asks the Buddha whether there is a self.

The Buddha remains silent.

Vacchagotta then asks whether there is no self.

Again, the Buddha remains silent.

He later explains to Ānanda that these answers risk entanglement in eternalism and annihilationism.

This does not negate the doctrine of anattā, but it complicates the common slogan that Buddhism simply asserts "there is no self."

Another translator, Bhikkhu Sujato, renders Vacchagotta's questions in terms of whether the self survives, highlighting their connection with the issue of existence after death. The precise interpretation is debated.

The philosophical issue is whether the framework of a separately existing entity is already mistaken before we ask whether that entity continues.

That is closely related to your observation about the laptop.

### Nāgārjuna: the radical nonduality you're reaching for

Nāgārjuna develops this line of inquiry in the Mūlamadhyamakakārikā.

The two most relevant passages are:

Chapter 24, verse 18: dependent origination is identified with emptiness and dependent designation.

Chapter 25, verses 19–20: saṃsāra and nirvāṇa are said not to possess an ultimate essential difference.

The second claim is especially close to your idea that liberation doesn't require discovering an entirely different universe.

But the philosophical point is emptiness of intrinsic nature, not that saṃsāra and nirvāṇa are identical in every practical, ethical or experiential respect.

There remains a meaningful conventional distinction between bondage and liberation.

One further complication: some Madhyamaka commentators understand these verses primarily as arguments about the inapplicability of metaphysical categories to both states, rather than simply as a declaration that everyday experience is already enlightenment.

So your interpretation has a real Mahāyāna foundation, but "nibbāna is already there underneath everything" is not a neutral summary of Buddhism as a whole.

## 4. Your laptop experiment already exists in early Buddhist philosophy

The closest canonical parallel is not merely Ñāṇananda. It is the chariot analogy, found in the Vajirā Sutta (SN 5.10).

The nun Vajirā is confronted by Māra, who questions the origin and existence of a being.

She replies:

> "Just as, with an assemblage of parts, the word 'chariot' is used"

She applies precisely the same reasoning to a person: the five aggregates provide the conventional basis for designating a being, without establishing an independent essence underneath them.

The five aggregates (pañcakkhandha) are form, feeling, perception, mental formations and consciousness.

Later, the Milindapañha expands this into an elaborate dialogue between King Milinda and the monk Nāgasena.

The king asks who Nāgasena really is. Nāgasena turns the question around and asks what makes the king's chariot a chariot.

Neither the wheels, axle, frame nor any other individual component is the chariot. Yet the assembled, functioning arrangement can correctly be called a chariot.

There are three interpretations worth distinguishing.

Mereological interpretation: No individual component is identical to the whole. This is an issue in the philosophy of parts and wholes.

Conventional designation: An object may exist conventionally through an arrangement of conditions without possessing an independently discoverable essence.

Buddhist not-self: The same investigation is extended to the supposed owner of bodily and psychological processes.

Notice that the argument doesn't establish that your laptop doesn't exist in any sense.

It challenges the type of existence you attribute to it.

This distinction is crucial because neither dependent origination nor emptiness implies that functioning objects are imaginary.

### A modern equivalent: Dennett's centre of narrative gravity

Daniel Dennett developed a fascinating account of selfhood in The Self as a Center of Narrative Gravity (1992).

Consider a physical object's centre of gravity. You can calculate its location and use it to predict behaviour. But you cannot remove the centre of gravity as a material component.

Dennett proposes something analogous for the self.

The self is a useful organisational abstraction through which we interpret a person's actions, statements, memories and intentions.

This is very close structurally to Nāgasena's chariot, though Dennett's argument arises from a different philosophical framework.

An even more radical contemporary theory appears in Thomas Metzinger's Being No One (2003).

Metzinger argues that the apparent self is an ongoing, transparent self-model. We experience the model's content without ordinarily experiencing the representational machinery that produces it.

That provides a remarkable meeting point:

| Thinker | What appears to be a self? |
|---|---|
| Vajirā / Nāgasena | A convention based on interacting aggregates |
| Ñāṇananda | A dependently maintained process of identification and becoming |
| Dennett | A centre of narrative gravity |
| Metzinger | The content of a transparent phenomenal self-model |
| Michael Levin | A functional agent defined by integrated goal-directed competencies |

They disagree about ontology, cognition and consciousness. But all challenge the idea that selfhood must be explained by discovering a simple, indivisible object somewhere inside the organism.

## 5. Michael Levin: the biological self as a collective intelligence

This is probably the richest scientific connection in your notes.

In his 2022 paper, Technological Approach to Mind Everywhere, Levin writes:

> "All known cognitive agents are collective intelligences"

His argument begins with an observation: every human starts as a single cell, develops into a vast multicellular organism, and eventually acquires complex behavioural and cognitive abilities.

Yet cognition doesn't suddenly become interesting only when neurons appear.

Cells already detect signals, regulate internal states, cooperate, respond to damage and contribute to collective biological objectives.

Levin calls his broader framework TAME — Technological Approach to Mind Everywhere.

### The bioelectricity isn't just metaphorical

Cells maintain electrical potential differences across their membranes using ion channels and transport mechanisms.

Through gap junctions and biochemical signalling, groups of cells coordinate activity.

These bioelectric networks can influence gene expression, developmental organisation, regeneration and anatomical outcomes.

The particularly striking result comes from planarian flatworms.

Experimental planaria can regenerate altered body plans following manipulation of bioelectric signalling.

Levin's group and collaborators showed that transient manipulation of intercellular signalling can produce two-headed planarians.

More importantly, subsequent amputations can produce two-headed animals again even after the original intervention is gone.

The result suggests that tissue can preserve a memory of its target anatomy without a corresponding permanent alteration to the DNA sequence.

Research also reports that this target morphology can be reset through further manipulation of bioelectric signalling.

This is a fundamentally different type of memory from remembering a childhood birthday.

It is a distributed regulatory state that influences how an organism reconstructs itself.

### Levin's cognitive light cone

His other concept is especially relevant to your proposed layers of selfhood.

The cognitive light cone describes the spatial and temporal scale of the states that an agent can detect, model and attempt to influence.

A cell may be concerned with local physiological stability.

A tissue can participate in the maintenance of an organ.

An organism can pursue goals involving its whole body, environment and future.

A human can imagine events decades ahead and coordinate plans involving thousands of other people.

Levin suggests that cognitive capacities can be compared across these scales rather than assigned a simple binary label of intelligent or unintelligent.

The interesting consequence is that there may be no single privileged scale at which biological agency begins.

### Where your idea goes beyond the evidence

Your proposed progression is:

Cells → bioelectric networks → collective goals → cognition → consciousness.

The first four elements have substantial conceptual and experimental support, although the interpretation of cellular behaviour as cognition remains debated.

The last transition is not scientifically established.

Levin has explored the possibility that sentience and cognition exist along broad continua. But demonstrating goal-directed behaviour or information processing does not demonstrate subjective experience.

A cell maintaining its membrane potential is not thereby proven to have a first-person perspective.

Likewise, visualising electrical gradients would show important biological mechanisms, not consciousness itself.

This is a particularly interesting contrast with Trika, where consciousness is metaphysically fundamental.

Levin is investigating how functional agencies arise and integrate. Trika addresses what consciousness ultimately is. Similar vocabulary can obscure radically different questions.

## 6. Aristotle's telos and the biology of purposes

You mentioned telos. The correct philosophical connection is Aristotle's theory of final causation.

In Aristotelian explanation, we can ask not only what physical events produced a structure but also what the structure is for.

A heart, for example, has a biological function. Its parts and activities can be understood in relation to the maintenance of an organism.

Crucially, Aristotle does not require a conscious designer who intended every natural purpose.

His biological teleology treats organisms as having natural ends.

Levin's investigations offer a possible modern parallel: cells coordinate their activity toward the recovery of particular anatomical outcomes.

But there's a distinction between:

- Teleology: explaining something through ends or purposes.
- Teleonomy: apparently purposeful organisation explained through biological mechanisms and evolutionary history.
- Intentional agency: an organism or system representing and pursuing goals.

Levin's work is interesting partly because it challenges us to ask whether these categories can be cleanly separated.

His developmental systems behave as though they are solving problems in anatomical space.

That doesn't establish Aristotelian metaphysics, but it gives you a striking correspondence between ancient philosophy and experimental developmental biology.

## 7. Bergson's theory of comedy is almost the reverse of Levin's biology

Henri Bergson published Laughter: An Essay on the Meaning of the Comic in 1900.

His central formula is:

> "Something mechanical encrusted on the living"

For Bergson, one major source of comedy is rigidity appearing where we expect flexibility.

A person behaves mechanically when the situation calls for adaptation.

Someone walks into a lamppost because they're distracted.

Someone keeps repeating an inappropriate response despite changing circumstances.

A pompous character follows a rule so rigidly that it becomes absurd.

Bergson sees laughter partly as a social correction of this inflexibility.

This gives a remarkable connection to your account of saṃsāra.

An identity can become ridiculous when its habitual behaviour continues operating after the conditions that originally made it useful have changed.

Imagine a man who learned as a child that every disagreement must be won through intimidation.

As an adult he attempts to use the same strategy during a trivial argument over restaurant seating.

The mechanism has become separated from intelligent adaptation.

That is simultaneously compatible with Bergson's account of comic rigidity and your proposed model of habitual becoming.

But the consequences need to be differentiated. The same conditioned rigidity may be comic in a harmless fictional situation and terrifying in a violent domestic situation.

For Bergson, emotional distance matters.

He even describes comedy as requiring:

> "a momentary anesthesia of the heart"

That's a powerful but debatable generalisation. Much comedy involves sympathy, tenderness and emotional involvement.

### Bergson actually provides three structural comic mechanisms

In Chapter II of Laughter, he identifies:

1. Repetition: the recurrence of a recognisable pattern.
2. Inversion: a familiar situation turned upside down.
3. Reciprocal interference of series: two independently coherent sequences becoming entangled.

The third is especially interesting for your theory of misdirection.

A situation is understood through one interpretive framework while the audience simultaneously recognises another.

The laughter arises from the interaction between them.

It also resembles the script opposition developed in Victor Raskin's Semantic Script Theory of Humor and extended by Salvatore Attardo.

In that framework, verbal humour often depends on an utterance being compatible with two opposed semantic interpretations.

### Bergson's other theory: memory

Bergson's Matter and Memory (1896) distinguishes habit memory from recollective memory.

Habit memory is acquired through repetition. It manifests in learned performance.

Recollective memory involves remembering particular past experiences as past.

Bergson illustrates this using the difference between knowing a passage by heart and remembering a particular occasion when you learned it.

This has a fascinating application to callbacks.

A comedian can exploit both the audience's learned familiarity with a repeated motif and its recollection of a specific earlier event.

The return of a line may feel satisfying because it has become recognisable, but it may become even more satisfying when its meaning changes in light of everything that happened afterwards.

Bergson doesn't offer a computational theory of callbacks. This is an interpretation suggested by his distinction.

### Aristotle's account of memory and time

In On Memory and Recollection, Aristotle states:

> "memory relates to the past."

He sharply distinguishes memory from present perception and future expectation.

That raises an interesting question about comedy: a callback is pleasurable partly because it integrates a present event with something the audience remembers from an earlier moment.

A punchline can also retrospectively alter the meaning of a setup.

So the audience is simultaneously holding a past interpretation, processing a present revelation and revising what it expects next.

This is a much richer temporal mechanism than merely placing a pause before a funny word.

## 8. Your information-theory connection has three serious theoretical foundations

There are already explicit attempts to explain humour as a form of computational learning.

### A. Shannon information theory: surprise as improbability

In classical information theory, the information content of an event can be expressed as:

I(x) = -log₂ P(x)

A highly probable event contains less Shannon information than an improbable one.

For example, in a simplified model:

| Probability of event | Information |
|---|---|
| 50% | 1 bit |
| 25% | 2 bits |
| 1% | 6.64 bits |
| 0.1% | 9.97 bits |

But this creates an immediate problem for comedy.

Random nonsense can be highly unpredictable without being funny.

So surprise, measured as improbability, is not sufficient to explain humour.

A meaningful punchline must also relate appropriately to the context and to the listener's interpretive machinery.

### B. Jürgen Schmidhuber: compression progress

This is arguably the closest established theory to your intuition.

In his 2009 work, Driven by Compression Progress, Jürgen Schmidhuber proposes that aesthetic interest arises when a learning system improves its ability to compress or predict structured data.

His theory explicitly discusses beauty, novelty, music, science and jokes.

The important idea is compression progress rather than simply compression.

A predictable sequence is easy to compress but might become boring.

Pure randomness is difficult to compress and may remain unintelligible.

A new pattern that suddenly becomes understandable can produce a burst of learning.

This is an excellent formal analogy to a punchline that makes sense of a previously ambiguous setup.

Your brain may suddenly discover a shorter or more coherent interpretation of the information.

The pleasure lies partly in the improvement of the model.

This is a theoretical proposal, not a demonstrated universal explanation of humour.

### C. Dennett, Hurley and Adams: laughter as error detection

Inside Jokes: Using Humor to Reverse-Engineer the Mind (2011), by Matthew Hurley, Daniel Dennett and Reginald Adams, offers another unusually close match.

Their proposal is that humour evolved in connection with detecting and correcting mistaken assumptions in thought.

A setup encourages an interpretation. A punchline exposes something wrong with it. Discovering the error produces an internal reward.

The theory attempts to explain why humans would find this sort of cognitive correction pleasurable.

Your idea of a comedian deliberately manipulating the audience's interpretive process fits this exceptionally well.

But it's controversial as a comprehensive theory. Some humour involves no obvious mistaken assumption, and laughter is not equivalent to detecting a successful correction.

### Does laughter measure how well information was compressed?

Not directly.

Laughter is an observable behavioural response, whereas successful comprehension, amusement, social bonding and cognitive model revision are distinct phenomena.

An audience might understand a joke completely and not laugh.

Someone might laugh socially without understanding it.

And laughter may sometimes be a response to embarrassment, nervousness or interpersonal tension.

So a comedian can use laughter as feedback about the performance, but it isn't a pure measurement of compression efficiency.

### D. Loewenstein: the information gap

George Loewenstein's 1994 theory of curiosity proposes that awareness of a gap in knowledge generates a desire to close it.

This explains something important about your setup-and-punchline idea.

The setup doesn't simply deliver information. It can make the audience conscious of something it doesn't yet know.

A mystery, joke or musical phrase can therefore create a kind of informational appetite.

The audience wants an answer.

There's a subtle implication.

The reward might come not just from the unexpected outcome, but from resolving a gap the audience has become invested in closing.

That explains why a completely unanticipated event can be less satisfying than a surprising conclusion to a carefully developed premise.

## 9. Your claim about evolution and novelty needs refining

Your intuition is that organisms seek novelty because novel information can help them survive.

There is genuine evolutionary and cognitive research supporting the usefulness of curiosity, exploration and information seeking.

But the claim that the most entertaining outcome is necessarily the most unexpected one is not supported.

Organisms balance exploration with exploitation.

Novelty can be useful, but predictable outcomes can also be rewarding. Familiar music, repeated jokes, stable social interactions and reliable environments can produce substantial pleasure.

Different mechanisms also contribute to reward.

Dopaminergic reward-prediction-error signalling helps explain forms of associative learning, but it does not mean dopamine simply measures surprise or humour.

This is also why the suggestion that we enjoy novelty only because it improves survival is too narrow. Human aesthetic pleasure may exploit learning mechanisms, social mechanisms, emotional regulation and other systems with different evolutionary histories.

Bergson, Dennett, Schmidhuber and Loewenstein therefore offer related but competing explanations, not interchangeable descriptions of one established mechanism.

## 10. Dharmakīrti and the supposed frame rate of consciousness

Dharmakīrti is extremely relevant, but the "frame rate" analogy needs careful treatment.

Dharmakīrti develops sophisticated arguments for kṣaṇikatva, the momentariness of existing phenomena.

One argument links existence to causal efficacy: things are real insofar as they can produce effects, and their moment-by-moment causal activity undermines the idea of permanent, unchanging substances.

What he does not provide is a scientifically measured temporal sampling rate for consciousness comparable to a video recorded at 24 or 60 frames per second.

Momentariness in Buddhist epistemology is a metaphysical and philosophical position.

Modern neuroscience investigates temporal integration, rhythmic attention and perceptual sampling, but these findings do not establish Dharmakīrti's doctrine.

There's another fascinating part of his thought, though: svasaṃvedana, or reflexive awareness.

This is awareness's capacity to be manifest to itself, not the assertion of a permanent substantial self.

The distinction between awareness being self-present and there being an enduring owner of awareness is important.

It connects directly with your intuition that investigation eventually turns toward the awareness involved in investigation.

But that intuition is not a logical theorem. It is a phenomenological possibility, and different philosophical traditions interpret it differently.

## 11. The Śiva Sūtras: a genuinely competing theory of the self

The opening aphorism of the Śiva Sūtras is:

Śiva Sūtra 1.1

चैतन्यमात्मा

Caitanyam ātmā

Consciousness is the Self.

This is not simply a more cheerful version of Buddhist not-self.

It's a fundamentally different metaphysical claim.

Classical nondual Śaivism identifies ultimate reality with consciousness, frequently understood as dynamic, self-aware and endowed with creative freedom.

In the Spanda tradition, consciousness is understood through spanda, its living dynamism or pulsation.

Mark Dyczkowski's The Doctrine of Vibration (1987) is especially useful here. He explains that the Spanda doctrine treats consciousness as active rather than merely a passive witness.

Importantly, spanda is not equivalent to neuronal electrical activity, electromagnetic frequencies, or measurable physical vibration.

It is a metaphysical account of conscious activity.

The next Śiva Sūtra is also interesting:

1.2 — Jñānaṃ bandhaḥ, commonly interpreted in this context as knowledge of differentiation or limited knowledge being bondage.

And 1.4 — Jñānādhiṣṭhānaṃ mātṛkā, which associates the power of letters or phonemic differentiation with the basis of limited knowledge.

These give you a more unexpected bridge to language.

Both Ñāṇananda and Kashmir Śaiva thinkers can be concerned with how conceptual differentiation structures experience.

But their ultimate accounts diverge.

For Ñāṇananda, the aim is to cease the proliferating, craving-driven structures that maintain bondage.

For Trika, the project involves recognition of consciousness's own nature and freedom.

The distinction between liberation through cessation and liberation through recognition is much more illuminating than saying one tradition teaches that you are nobody and the other teaches that you are God.

## 12. Bergson's duration actually conflicts with the frame-rate model

One further contrast is worth highlighting.

In Bergson's philosophy, lived time—durée—is not adequately represented as a succession of independent, measurable instants.

The past permeates the present through memory. Conscious experience is qualitative and continuous rather than simply a sequence of static frames.

This contrasts with a strongly atomistic interpretation of Buddhist momentariness.

That creates a genuine philosophical problem:

If consciousness consists of momentary events, how is the continuity of experience explained?

If consciousness is intrinsically continuous, how should we understand apparent moments of discontinuity, interruption and transformation?

Different schools respond differently.

And this is directly relevant to your larger claim about identity persisting through time.

## 13. Baudrillard, simulation and the possibility that signs create worlds

Jean Baudrillard's Simulacra and Simulation explores situations in which signs no longer function simply as representations of an independent original.

His concept of hyperreality concerns a world increasingly organised through images, models and representations that can supersede the things they're ostensibly representing.

Your MacBook example can connect to this at the level of branding, symbolic value and mediated perception.

A physical computer has material properties and functions.

But a particular branded object also carries an elaborate symbolic identity involving status, marketing, desire, narratives and social recognition.

The symbolic object can become more important to a consumer than many of its physical properties.

However, Baudrillard is not straightforwardly proposing that reality runs inside a computer simulation.

That is a different argument, associated with thinkers such as Nick Bostrom.

Likewise, Buddhist emptiness does not entail that the external physical world is merely a computer-generated illusion.

There are useful structural similarities, but three distinct propositions must remain separate: objects are conceptually designated, experience is representationally mediated, and the physical universe is simulated by another computer.

## 14. The most interesting additional connections I found

### Hume's bundle theory

David Hume argued that introspection never reveals an independently enduring self underneath perception.

Instead, we encounter changing perceptions associated through memory, resemblance and causal relationships.

His bundle theory is an unusually direct Western philosophical parallel to Buddhist not-self.

Interestingly, Hume later expressed dissatisfaction with aspects of his own account of personal identity, especially the difficulty of explaining relations between distinct perceptions.

This means the absence of a discoverable permanent self does not automatically solve the problem of continuity.

### Levin versus Metzinger: can a self be both constructed and real?

This is an important tension that your current notes don't fully explore.

Metzinger questions whether a self exists as an entity at all.

Levin deliberately defines selves functionally, in terms of their integrated capacities, goals and boundaries.

From Levin's perspective, a multicellular agent can be meaningfully real at a particular scale without being composed of an indivisible substance.

This suggests a distinction between having a functional identity and being a permanent metaphysical subject.

A self could be an emergent organisational achievement without being an eternal owner.

That may be a more productive synthesis than the simple opposition between self and no-self.

### Active inference: the system that keeps trying to secure its future

Karl Friston and collaborators' active-inference framework investigates how biological systems maintain themselves through perception, action and regulation.

Organisms can act in ways that bring sensory states into correspondence with preferred or expected conditions.

The framework connects predictive processing with action and homeostatic control.

This provides a possible scientific analogy to your picture of an organism continually seeking conditions it predicts will make it secure or satisfied.

But active inference is not a scientific validation of Buddhist craving or nibbāna.

A homeostatically successful organism need not be psychologically liberated, and reducing prediction error is not identical to eliminating suffering.

### Addiction as maladaptive learned persistence

Your example of a person repeatedly following a harmful strategy has a strong parallel in addiction neuroscience.

Repeated drug exposure can alter learning, reward processing, incentive motivation and habitual behaviour.

Some mechanisms help explain why learned patterns continue even after their harmful consequences become apparent.

This gives empirical substance to your broader analogy between conditioned habits and repetitive suffering.

But drug dependence is not simply a philosophical failure to recognise no-self. It is a complex medical and behavioural phenomenon.

### A surprising parallel between memory and karma

Bergson's habit memory, Levin's morphological memory and Buddhist karmic conditioning can all be examined as different ways the past constrains the present.

They are not the same physical or metaphysical process.

But they raise a common question:

How can something persist and exert causal influence when none of its constituent states remains unchanged?

That's a fundamental problem shared by biology, memory research, theories of selfhood and Buddhist philosophy.
