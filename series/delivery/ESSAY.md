# THE INSTRUMENT — full script

> Episode 9, Fundamentals of Comedy. ~14 min (840s board).

> Thesis: Delivery is the instrument — voice, pace, energy, body. Timing is the score;
> delivery is what plays it.

---

**[0:00] ENGINE NAMED.** Last week was the silence between the notes. This
week is the voice that plays inside it. Same words, different comic, different
room — and only one version gets the laugh. That difference has a name.
Delivery is the instrument: voice, pace, energy, body. Timing will tell us
when to play. Delivery decides whether anyone hears it.

**[0:30] PROOF.** Listen to two ends of the same spectrum. Norm Macdonald,
never stopping, words tumbling like he is outrunning the thought. [CLIP]
Then Stewart Lee, so slow the room starts checking the exits. [CLIP] Both
rooms are roaring. Neither man could do the other's job with the other's
voice. The words alone explain nothing here. The instrument explains
everything.

Here is the number, on screen, in gold: working comics span nearly eighty
words a minute, fastest to slowest. [GOLD CARD: 192 → 117 wpm, Norm → Lee —
measured, method in resources/delivery/DATA.md] Same language. Nearly double
the speed. Both funny on purpose. Delivery is not decoration on the joke.
Delivery is half the joke wearing a different outfit.

**[1:30] THESIS + VOCABULARY.** Delivery does three jobs. One: it carries
energy — and energy is presence, not volume. The room feels held, not
shouted at. Two: it embodies characters. Every act-out asks the room to
believe something impossible, and the room only pays if you pay first. Three:
it lands jokes. It goes slow enough to let them land. It never steps on the
release.

Ten words for this series, then no new jargon: delivery, presence, commit,
pace, energy, act-out, persona, mic, stage, land. That is the whole shelf.
The rest is demonstration.

**[2:30] LENS (music first).** A violin and a trumpet can play the same
notes and make different weather. Nobody confuses them. Nobody says the
trumpet is the violin played louder. The tone IS the message before a single
phrase resolves. Ask a guitarist what changes when they switch from fingers
to a pick. Everything. Same strings. Different hands.

Comedy works the same way. Two comics can say the same sentence and only one
gets the laugh, because the sentence was never the whole signal. The voice
carrying it — its weight, its speed, its stillness — tells the room how to
receive every word inside it. [GOLD CARD: instrument ≠ score] You can write
the perfect score. Played on a broken instrument, it stays silent.

**[4:30] PERSONA.** Some comics arrive loud and learn to be present. Early on,
one working comic masked every insecurity with an over-the-top angry
delivery — in your face, no smile, no fun. [CLIP: interview] The room heard
the mask before the joke. Six years of nights peeled it off. The instrument
got quieter and the rooms got louder.

Presence is not volume. Here is the test working comics pass around: record
one set, watch it back with the sound off. Can you still tell the room is
held? If yes, that is presence. If the whole thing goes flat without decibels,
that was never energy. That was noise wearing energy's jacket.

Then there is commitment. Act-outs are the biggest ask in comedy. You want
a room full of strangers to believe you are two people arguing in a kitchen. Go all
in or cut it. [CLIP: full-commit act-out] The room senses the hedge instantly
and checks out. Half belief buys zero laughs.

And none of this is natural. "Natural" is dozens of faces in a bedroom mirror
before one face on stage. [GOLD CARD: mirror hours] Every gesture you think
was improvised was rehearsed until it looked improvised. That is not fakery.
That is respect for the room.

**[6:00] CASE STUDY (one held voice, start to finish).** Watch a comic hold a
single borrowed voice for a full minute and never drop it. [CLIP ×4] Greg
Davies, doing his Ed Gamble voice. Same joke engine underneath — a story
about a friend — but the entire comedy rides on the voicing. Pitch sits
higher. Vowels go rounder. The sentences get fussier, more outraged, more
precise. He never winks. He never apologizes for the voice. That refusal is
the joke's floor.

Now watch what the body does while the voice works. Still mic. Free hands.
[CLIP] Every gesture belongs to the character, not the actor. Nothing fidgets.
The technique is invisible, which is exactly why the character is visible.
Compare that to the failure reel every open-mic host knows: mic swapping
hands, finger on the grille, pacing like a hostage. [GOLD CARD: distraction
tax] Each fidget spends attention the joke needed.

Then the landing. The bit ends and he comes out of the voice cleanly — one
beat. Back to himself. Next setup. [CLIP] No stepping on the laugh. No rush
into the next premise. The joke lands because he gave it somewhere to land.
Delivery did all three jobs in sixty seconds: held the room, embodied the
man, landed the thing.

**[9:00] INSIDE THE MINUTE (case-study coda).** Slow that minute down and
every choice is load-bearing. Sentence one arrives already inside the voice —
no announcement, no throat-clearing. The room meets the character before the
premise. Sentence two raises the fussiness exactly one notch. Sentence three
holds the pitch while the hands stay dead still, and the contrast between a
wild voice and a calm body is where the laugh starts leaking early. Those are
laughs in anticipation of a punchline not yet said — the audience can feel how
committed this is, and commitment is contagious.

The alternative ending proves it. Imagine the same story told straight, in his
own voice, same words. [BEAT: flat read] Nothing. The premise is fine. The
structure is fine. The room stays quiet. The words were never the engine here.
The voice was. Change the instrument, keep the score, lose the song.

**[10:00] SECOND SPECIMEN.** Same function, opposite handling. Adam Bloom,
live in a small room, obsessing over single syllables out loud. [CLIP] Where
Davies goes big and borrowed, Bloom goes small and exact — turning words over
in front of you, tasting them, rejecting one — keeping another. Two comics.
Two opposite instruments. Same job: make the room trust the voice enough to
follow it anywhere.

**[10:45] THE SMALL ROOM (specimen coda).** Bloom's version works because the
room is close. You can hear consonants click. [CLIP: close syllable
work] In a theatre that fussiness dies halfway back. Davies's voice fills a
theatre; Bloom's fills a back room. Neither is better. Each is tuned to its
room. [GOLD CARD: instrument tuned to room] Delivery is not one skill. It is a
setting on a dial, and the room picks the number.

**[11:30] FAILURE MODE.** What happens when the instrument is out of tune.
First failure: rushing. The comic machine-guns setups, sprints past every
laugh, steps on each release before it opens. [CLIP ×2, rushed set] The room
wanted to laugh and was never given the gap. Rushing reads as fear, and fear
is the one thing rooms never laugh at.

Second failure: the hedge. An act-out starts, the comic feels the room
wobble, and pulls back halfway — voice drops, body apologizes. [CLIP] The
room senses the withdrawal and follows it out the door. Third failure: mic
fidgets. Pacing. A finger on the grille. Every one spends attention. Add enough
of them and the joke arrives bankrupt. The words were fine. The instrument
ate them.

**[12:45] UNIVERSAL.** Every room you have ever stood in was played, not just
told. The classroom where one teacher held you silent. The interview where
your own voice went thin. The first date where you talked too fast and knew
it while doing it. Same sentences. Another instrument. Another night.
Stages just charge admission for a lesson you already know: people do not
respond to what you say half as much as to the voice saying it.

**[13:30] CLOSE + HOOK.** Delivery is the instrument — voice, pace, energy,
body. It carries the charge, becomes the people, and gives every laugh
somewhere to land. Three jobs, one player, no score yet. The instrument is tuned. Next week: the score — when to play what.
