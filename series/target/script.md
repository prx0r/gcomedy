## [1:30] THESIS + VOCABULARY

Targeting does three jobs, and this video checks all three on every bit. It names the butt first — powerful, self, or species, decided before writing, never discovered after. It matches assumed to intended — the room assigns the target automatically, from the setup and your face, and your intention is not admissible evidence. And it holds one butt per joke — diffuse blame is diffuse laughter. Words for this video, and only these: target, butt, self, powerful, species, victim, veto, aim, liability, ledger. ## [6:00] CASE STUDY

One audit, line by line — the fence permit. A comic spent four months getting a fence permit and told the table about it. The draft went like this: my friend waited four months, I would've built the fence. Freeze it. Whoever the setup holds is who the punch happens to — and the setup is holding the friend, not the office. The room hears a line about a chump, whatever the teller intended. Now the repair, three moves. Move one: replant the setup — commit the room to a belief about the process, not the person. Move two: rule of three — forty chairs, one clerk, a screen that says you're next; two of those are decoration. Move three: end on the replacing word. Result: the office needed four months to decide a fence was a fence. Same observation. Same attitude. Different butt — and the friend becomes a co-author of the complaint instead of its subject.

## [9:00] CASE PAYOFF

Run the ledger on that repair, because this is the instrument the video leaves you with. Every joke pays somebody; write down who, weekly, in one column. The draft's column said: friend. The repair's column says: office. Patterns emerge fast — always-safe targets mean cowardice, always-victims mean cruelty, and most comics are shocked by their own column within a month. The butt ledger doesn't make you kinder. It makes you legible — to yourself first, then to the room. Carlin's "stuff" bit survives forty years because its ledger column says "everyone, including me." Nobody can file a complaint against everyone. That's not writing. That's the cheapest laugh at the table, bought fair.

## [10:45] SPECIMEN TURN

And here's the veto from the other side — when the room revokes a license you used to hold. Rock's twelve-minute routine didn't change a word between 1996 and 2005. The assignment changed: what the room heard the joke to be on drifted, year by year, from the behavior to the people — and the author pulled the bit rather than re-aim it. "I've never done that joke again, ever." No rewrite. No defense. A retirement.

## [11:30] FAILURE MODE

Three deaths, and the confessional kid should read this twice. One: punch-down dressed as edge — loud violation, unclear butt, the comic mistaking volume for aim. Carr's rule, from his own mouth: he doesn't know where the mark is until he oversteps it — but he writes down every step, which is why he survives the steps. Two: diffuse butt — everyone slightly insulted, nobody laughing, the ledger column reading "miscellaneous." If two candidates, pick one; make the other a tag. Three: room-as-butt overused — roast-the-room works once per set, max. The second time the shared enemy is just the people who paid, and that's not a bit, that's a billing dispute. No number in this beat. Failures you recognize; only machinery gets counted.

## [12:45] UNIVERSAL

Every joke has a butt — and so does every argument you've ever lost, every review you've ever received, every family story that starts "remember when." Naming the butt is honesty: it says who pays for the laugh before the laugh is spent. People who learn to name their targets on stage start noticing unnamed targets everywhere else — the meeting where the joke was on the intern, the thread where it was on nobody and therefore everybody. A named butt is a fair fight. An unnamed one is just bleeding the room and calling it comedy.
