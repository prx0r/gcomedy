# TARGET: WHO YOU AIM AT — full script

> Episode 12, Fundamentals of Comedy. ~14 min (840s board).

---

**[0:00] ENGINE NAMED.** Positions were set last week — you know where
everyone stands. Now the only question that matters: who are you aiming
at? Every joke has a butt. Someone pays for the laugh. Name them before
you write a word — the powerful, yourself, everyone at once — and never
the victim. The target decides the safety math: who you attack determines
whether the room laughs, groans, or files a complaint with the bartender.
This video is about the butt.

**[0:30] PROOF.** One joke, two butts, two rooms. [CLIP: Tomlinson, "fire
sale, everything goes" — then the same shape aimed outward at a named
patient.] Aim the diagnosis at herself and the room leans in — she's
paying, we're guests. Aim the identical observation at someone in the
crowd and watch the laugh change shape: faster, shorter, nobody adds to
it, topic changes within two sentences. Same words. Opposite butt.
Opposite weather. One number in this beat: two laughs, same joke — count
the tags after each. The self-aimed one grows tags. The outward one kills
them. [GOLD CARD: hesitation-shape coding PENDING at build.]

**[1:30] THESIS + VOCABULARY.** Targeting does three jobs, and
this video checks all three on every bit. It names the butt first —
powerful, self, or species, decided before writing, never discovered
after. It matches assumed to intended — the room assigns the target
automatically, from the setup and your face, and your intention is not
admissible evidence. And it holds one butt per joke — diffuse blame is
diffuse laughter. Words for this video, and only these: target, butt,
self, powerful, species, victim, veto, aim, liability, ledger. Ten terms,
in the description. Hierarchy words belong to last week; they visit, they
don't move in.

**[2:30] LENS (satire first).** Every era has a song about who the song is
on. The court jester sang up — at the crown, at the court, never at the
kitchen boy. The protest singer names the institution, not the intern.
Satire that aims down has a different name, and it's not satire — it's a
dismissal notice with a rhyme scheme. The rule predates microphones by
centuries: the powerful can afford the joke, the victim can't afford the
agreement. r/Standup relitigates this monthly — 207 comments last winter,
both sides quoting the same dead geniuses — because the *feeling* is real
even when the slogan is sloppy. [GOLD CARD: direction-price table — laughing
at the powerful costs the room nothing; laughing at the vulnerable costs
visible agreement.] The number for this beat: four chairs. Powerful, self,
species, victim. Three you may sit in. One is wired to an alarm.

**[4:30] PERSONA.** Same line, opposite mouths. The fixer walks on stage
holding a clipboard — everything he says lands on systems, institutions,
the broken machine, because the room read the uniform before the first
word. The victim walks on holding a wound — the identical sentence lands
on a person, because the room reads the wound first. [CLIP: Gervais,
Globes — "I don't care anymore" — the room IS the powerful, and the
tuxedos are the uniform that makes every jab land upward.] Then the
confessional: Tomlinson's bipolar material, aimed at herself with the
earnest frame on — "fire sale, everything goes" — which is why a room of
strangers laughs *with* a diagnosis instead of flinching at it. [GOLD
CARD: confessional +0.2 working threshold, CALIBRATE.] One number here:
one line, two mouths. Know which mouth you're in before you open it.

**[6:00] CASE STUDY.** One audit, line by line — the fence permit. A comic
spent four months getting a fence permit and told the table about it. The
draft went like this: *my friend waited four months, I would've built the
fence.* Freeze it. Whoever the setup holds is who the punch happens to —
and the setup is holding the friend, not the office. The room hears a line
about a chump, whatever the teller intended. Now the repair, three moves.
Move one: replant the setup — commit the room to a belief about the
*process*, not the person. Move two: rule of three — forty chairs, one
clerk, a screen that says you're next; two of those are decoration. Move
three: end on the replacing word. Result: the office needed four months to
decide a fence was a fence. Same observation. Same attitude. Different
butt — and the friend becomes a co-author of the complaint instead of its
subject. [CLIP: Carlin, "a house is just a place for your stuff while you
go out and get more stuff" — setup holds everyone, punch happens to
species.]

**[9:00] CASE PAYOFF (case coda).** Run the ledger on that repair, because this is the
instrument the video leaves you with. Every joke pays somebody; write down
who, weekly, in one column. The draft's column said: friend. The repair's
column says: office. Patterns emerge fast — always-safe targets mean
cowardice, always-victims mean cruelty, and most comics are shocked by
their own column within a month. The butt ledger doesn't make you kinder.
It makes you *legible* — to yourself first, then to the room. Carlin's
"stuff" bit survives forty years because its ledger column says
"everyone, including me." Nobody can file a complaint against everyone.
That's not writing. That's the cheapest laugh at the table, bought fair.

**[10:00] SECOND SPECIMEN.** Same device, different handling: self-target,
earnest versus shield. Specimen A, confessional: Tomlinson's arm-floaties
bit — the diagnosis aimed at herself, the frame earnest, the room granted
permission because she paid first and visibly. [CLIP: T1, paused on "they
have arm floaties."] Specimen B, shield: the identical target — herself,
same diagnosis — delivered performative, deflecting, the self-lowering
timed to dodge the *next* joke's real butt. Watch the room's hands: the
confessional gets tags, additions, the audience building on it. The shield
gets the short laugh, no tags, topic change. One number for the specimen
beat: two frames, one target. Earnest buys distance. Performative spends it.

**[10:45] SPECIMEN TURN (specimen coda).** And here's the veto from the other side — when
the room revokes a license you used to hold. Rock's twelve-minute routine
didn't change a word between 1996 and 2005. The *assignment* changed: what
the room heard the joke to be on drifted, year by year, from the behavior
to the people — and the author pulled the bit rather than re-aim it.
"I've never done that joke again, ever." No rewrite. No defense. A
retirement. [GOLD CARD: veto-gate overlay — victim butt, no frame, FAIL.]
The turn the film wants you to feel: target is not a property of the text.
It's a property of the contract. Same text, new contract, no joke.

**[11:30] FAILURE MODE.** Three deaths, and the confessional kid should
read this twice. One: punch-down dressed as edge — loud violation, unclear
butt, the comic mistaking volume for aim. Carr's rule, from his own mouth:
he doesn't know where the mark is until he oversteps it — but he writes
down every step, which is why he survives the steps. Two: diffuse butt —
everyone slightly insulted, nobody laughing, the ledger column reading
"miscellaneous." If two candidates, pick one; make the other a tag. Three:
room-as-butt overused — roast-the-room works once per set, max. The second
time the shared enemy is just the people who paid, and that's not a bit,
that's a billing dispute. No number in this beat. Failures you recognize;
only machinery gets counted.

**[12:45] UNIVERSAL.** Every joke has a butt — and so does every
argument you've ever lost, every review you've ever received, every family
story that starts "remember when." Naming the butt is honesty: it says who
pays for the laugh before the laugh is spent. People who learn to name
their targets on stage start noticing unnamed targets everywhere else —
the meeting where the joke was on the intern, the thread where it was on
nobody and therefore everybody. A named butt is a fair fight. An unnamed
one is just bleeding the room and calling it comedy.

**[13:30] CLOSE + HOOK.** The target decides the safety math. Name the butt first —
powerful, self, species. Match assumed to intended. Hold one butt — the ledger keeps you honest. Victims never pay — that's the veto, and the
veto is final. Butt named. Next week: the path you lead them down —
misdirection.
